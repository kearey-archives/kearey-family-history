VIII. The Dublin and London Keareys: A Parallel Family History (1791–Present)

Genealogical status. The Dublin–London Kearey family is documented separately from the County Meath Keary families. A possible relationship remains a research hypothesis; no direct genealogical link has yet been established. Similar surnames and historical circumstances do not, by themselves, demonstrate descent.

1. Origins in Dublin and the Decision to Leave (c. 1791–1816)

Terence Kearey's family research identifies Thomas Kearey (1791–1860) as the starting point for the documented London line. In Ciardha/Kearey: Rebirth of an Ancient Irish Clan, Terence reports a baptism at St Paul's parish church, Dublin, on 4 December 1791, naming James and Mary Keary as parents; he also cites British census records recording Thomas's Irish birthplace. Thomas is described as settling in London by the time of his marriage in 1819. Accounts differ on the exact year of his departure from Ireland, and the reasons attributed to him in the historical novel The London Keareys should not be mistaken for independently documented personal motives.

Occupations in London. Terence's research describes Thomas in census and death records as a baggage worker and as a gold-and-silver smelter or refiner. His exact employment sequence and workplaces require confirmation from the individual records. Terence notes that searches of published goldsmith and apprenticeship rolls did not establish a corresponding entry for Thomas; the broader description of him as a goldsmith should therefore not be presented as an independently confirmed trade qualification.

2. Why “Kearey”? The Anglicisation of the Name

The surname appears as Kearey in English records from the 1820s onward. The precise reasons for this spelling are uncertain. The following are possible influences, not established explanations for this particular family, and spelling similarity does not prove a connection with the County Meath Kearys:

  • Clerical orthography. Different clerks and record-keepers may have represented similar-sounding surnames using different spellings, including Keary and Kearey.
  • Consistent record-keeping. A spelling adopted in trade, parish or civil records may have become established through repeated use; there is no direct evidence here that the family deliberately chose it for occupational reasons.
  • Social context. Irish migrants encountered different linguistic and social conventions in London. Whether those circumstances influenced this family’s surname spelling remains unknown.

3. Five Generations in Outline (1791 → Present)

  • (I) Thomas Kearey (1791–1860): Dublin-born ancestor of the documented London line; identified in Terence Kearey's research with a baptism naming James and Mary Keary. Married Ester (also written Esther) Pepler in 1819 at St Anne's, Soho. Census and death records are described as recording his work in baggage handling and metal smelting or refining.
  • (II) Thomas Kearey (c.1820–1867): London-born son of Thomas and Ester. According to Terence's family research and Albert's recollections, he worked as a whitesmith and married Hannah Raybould. The family accounts vary slightly in recorded birth years and spellings.
  • (III) Alfred Kearey (1854–1917): House painter and decorator, married Martha Sutton. Albert Edward Kearey's autobiographical account, reproduced by Terence, records Alfred's death at home on 20 December 1917, while Albert was serving in France—not death in combat. The family's wartime losses included four sons: Walter Ambrose, Sidney Reginald, Alfred Ernest and Frederick Augustus.
  • (IV) Albert Edward Kearey (1889–1971): Served with the 13th Battalion, London Regiment (Kensingtons) from youth; rose to Sergeant Major in the First World War and was awarded the Distinguished Conduct Medal (D.C.M.) for gallantry; later commissioned Major in the Territorial Army during the Second World War (Home Defence, North London); after the war worked as Cartage Manager with the London and North Eastern Railway (LNER).
  • (V) Terence Arthur Kearey (b. 1935): Son of Albert Edward Kearey and Elsie May Collins; author and compiler of the London family and wider Kearey clan research.

Interpretation. The Dublin–London family narrative illustrates how employment, migration and changing social circumstances could shape successive generations of an Irish-origin family. The First World War losses form an important part of the account. The surname’s recorded spelling and the family’s London history are relevant to questions of identity, but neither establishes descent from the County Meath Kearys.

4. Social, Religious, and Labour Context in London (1816–1971)

Religious life. The religious history of the London family should not be inferred from the Catholic identity of the County Meath farmers. In The London Keareys, Terence portrays Thomas as having been raised Protestant; because that work is a historical novel, the portrayal requires confirmation from church records. A Church of Ireland baptism is reported in his separate family research. Albert Edward Kearey's autobiography specifically recalls attendance at Sunday school and St Matthew's Church, St Petersburg Place, Kensington, during his childhood. These sources do not support a blanket description of the London generations as a continuously Catholic family.

Work and mobility. The family records describe work in baggage handling, metal refining and whitesmithing, followed by house painting and later railway management. They also document changing addresses and circumstances in London. These occupational developments can be compared with wider patterns of urban employment without assigning every family member to the same religious community or assuming a single uninterrupted progression through the trades.

Identity. Across successive generations, the London family became established in English social and economic life. Its history provides a useful comparison with the County Meath families, but the precise relationship between the two remains an open genealogical question.
