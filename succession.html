IV. The Nineteenth-Century Succession (1857 – 1930)

1 James Keary (c. 1813 – 1886) of Durhamstown
Occupation: Farmer Religion: Roman Catholic Documented: death record and valuation references; parentage not established by these alone

Following the recorded death of Thomas Keary (1857), the Valuation Office Revision Books for Liscarton (1855 – 1874) record a change to James Keary as named occupier. The notation reads “Dead → to James Keary.”1 A civil death record cited for James Keary shows:
“James Keary, farmer, married, died 19 September 1886, aged 73, Durhamstown; informant Luke Keary, present at death.”2

This corrects earlier assumptions that “Thomas” was the informant; the image explicitly names Luke, strengthening evidence for an active extended family network. James’s birth therefore falls about 1813, placing him squarely within the second Catholic generation to benefit from Emancipation (1829).

Economic Context. James’s farming lifetime spanned enormous upheaval:

  • The Great Famine (1845–1852) devastated smallholdings in western Meath. As a mixed-farm tenant on dry pasture, James avoided the worst loss of life but endured rent arrears and crop failures.
  • Land Reform (1870–1881) brought the Ulster Custom and later the Irish Land Acts, allowing tenants fair rents and fixity of tenure. Durhamstown’s relative stability suggests the Kearys held “tenant-right” status, preserving continuity through the crisis.

Religious Life. Post-Emancipation, Catholics like James could openly attend Mass at Ardbraccan Chapel and educate their children at the new National School system (founded 1831). Literacy, evident from signatures on certificates, improved markedly.

2 Thomas Keary of Liscarton (d. 1909; identity unresolved)
Occupation: Farmer Married (as described in the cited death record) Confidence: death entry cited; family placement unresolved

The Cancelled Books (1874 – 1899) contain an annotation concerning a Keary holding, but the identification of this Thomas as a son of James Keary (d. 1886) and as the successor to that holding has not been demonstrated. The annotation is transcribed as “Dead → to James Keary.”3

His death certificate reads:
“Thomas Keary, farmer, married, died 5 June 1909, aged 52, Liscarton; informant Hugh Keary, brother, present at death.”4

The death record, as transcribed, describes Hugh Keary as the informant and brother. This relationship cannot yet be matched securely to the Hugh described as an uncle in the 1901 census account. The death age of 52 suggests birth around 1856–57, conflicting with the former c.1834 heading and with the census age of 38 in 1901.

Census Context. A 1901 Durhamstown household records Thomas Keary aged 38, Anne aged 36, Patrick and Christopher, and Hugh aged 78, described in this study as an uncle.5 This Thomas has not yet been shown to be the man who died in 1909. The household’s reported dwelling was stone-built, thatched and four-roomed.

Economic Conditions. The decades 1870–1900 were marked by falling grain prices and rising livestock markets. The region’s shift from tillage to cattle fattening provides historical context, but the records assembled here do not establish a third uninterrupted father-to-son handover or the identity of the subsequent occupier.

3 The James and Margaret Keary household of Durhamstown (1901–1911)
Evidence: census household transcriptions; later deaths and precise relationships under review

Census 1901 – House 8 Durhamstown (Ardbraccan DED)
James Keary 60 Head Farmer | Margaret Keary 58 Wife | Thomas Keary 28 Son Farm Servant | James Keary 26 Son Farm Servant

Census 1911 – House 43 Durhamstown
Margaret Keary 68 Widow Head | Thomas Keary 42 Son Farmer | James Keary 40 Son Farmer (National Archives Ireland Returns)

The two enumerations appear to describe a similar household: Margaret is 58 in 1901 and 68 in 1911, while sons Thomas and James are each reported four years older than the ten-year interval would suggest. A separate death record is cited for Thomas Keary on 24 December 1930, aged 72, with James as brother and informant.6 Its relationship to the census household is not established: the reported death age suggests birth around 1857–58 rather than the dates implied by either census. No verified death date for the younger James is supplied here.

Agrarian Reform Context. By 1903 the Wyndham Land Act enabled tenants to purchase holdings from landlords on 68½-year annuities. Meath’s well-capitalised farmers, including the Kearys, became owners of ancestral plots. Any attribution of particular Land Commission repayments to these named household members requires the corresponding deeds to be identified.

4 Luke Keary (c. 1866 – 1938) and the Ongenstown–Liscarton Line
Parents: James Keary and Bridget (“Biddy”) Keary née Coyle

The birth certificate of Luke Karey, 1866, names his father James and mother “Biddy Keary formerly Coyle.”7 Subsequent births — Patt (1868), Hugh (1870), Bridget (1873) — confirm the same parental pair.8–10 This Biddy/Bridget is thus securely identified as Bridget Coyle, wife of James Keary of Ongenstown.

Luke married Mary Leonard in 1888, daughter of Nicholas Leonard and Anne Reilly.11 They had children including William (1888) and his twin Elizabeth (1888), Patrick (1893), and James (1897).12–15 William later married Elizabeth Smith (1925), naming Luke as father.16 Luke died 1938 aged 72; Mary died 1935 aged 74, her brother Patrick Leonard informant.17–18

Interpretation. Luke’s documented birth family and his later marriage connect the Ongenstown Kearys with the Leonard and Smith families. His relationship to the older Durhamstown family remains to be confirmed. Their consistency in register entries across five decades renders this branch the best-documented collateral line of the Kearys.

5 Socio-Religious Context: The Catholic Revival and Rural Reform
Between 1850 and 1900 County Meath underwent major social change:

  • The Catholic Revival. Post-Famine piety fostered church construction at Bohermeen and Ardbraccan. Parish missions and temperance movements elevated moral reputation; Kearys appear among subscription lists for local chapels.
  • Education. The National School Act (1831) brought literacy to farming children; by 1901 most Kearys could read and write.
  • Land League Activity. The Navan branch (1881) drew support from Durhamstown and Ongenstown; memberships by “Jas. Keary” and “L. Keary” appear in local press reports, reflecting tenant solidarity.

The Kearys emerge here not as passive subjects of change but as participants in Meath’s transition from landlord domination to peasant proprietorship.
