IX. Economic, Religious, and Cultural Commentary (1730–1978): What Was Happening When Our People Lived It

1. The Penal Era to Emancipation (c. 1730–1830)

Historical context. Landholding, religious restrictions and education changed significantly during the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. The Martry memorial identifies named individuals and dates, but does not itself establish their denomination, leasehold status or literacy.

Markets and flax. A James Keary is indexed in the 1796 flax-growers list at Martry. The index establishes a named association with the locality but not James’s relationship to Luke or any particular property.

2. The Long Nineteenth Century (1830–1911): Famine, Reform, Purchase

Famine impact. County Meath experienced the effects of the Great Famine. The records examined for this study do not establish particular Keary households’ losses, acquisitions or farming changes during the period.

The Land War and Acts. The 1870 and 1881 Land Acts and the 1903 Wyndham Act changed the legal framework for tenant farming. The 1911 census records Rose and members of her household but does not establish ownership of their property. Ownership claims require deeds or valuation evidence.

Religion and education. Church life and schooling changed during the nineteenth century. An individual’s appearance as a civil-registration informant or witness does not alone establish their education, religious practice or broader social standing.

3. The Free State Era and After (1922–1978): Stability and Closure

Agriculture. Farming remained important in interwar County Meath. The cited records do not establish the agricultural practices, ownership arrangements or income of the specific Keary households discussed here.

Demography and remembrance. Memorial inscriptions at Bohermeen and Martry document individuals commemorated in these places. Burial proximity does not establish that they belonged to one descendant line, nor that particular households were childless.

A later Martry inscription commemorates Patrick Keary (d. 1955), James Keary (d. 1963), Mary Keary (d. 1967), Betty Keary (d. 1970) and William Keary (d. 1978). The cited birth records separately identify William (1888), Patrick (1893) and James (1897) as children of Luke Keary and Mary formerly Leonard. The inscription alone does not establish the birth years or parentage of everyone commemorated, or a descent from Luke who died in 1806.1

Memory. The records presented here document the County Meath Kearys into the late twentieth century. Separately, the Dublin–London Kearey family is traced through later generations to Terence Arthur Kearey (b. 1935). A direct genealogical connection between the two histories has not been established; considered together, they offer contrasting perspectives on family continuity and adaptation.

Compiler’s Note

Irish civil records occasionally show variant spellings (e.g., Carey for Keary; Kearey in London); identification rests on place, informants/witnesses, and kin recurrence more than on orthography alone. Ages on Irish certificates often vary by ±5–10 years; where conflict arises, contemporary events (marriage/children) and informant identity carry greatest weight.
