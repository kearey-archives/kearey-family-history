IV. The Nineteenth-Century Succession (1857 – 1930)

1 James Keary of Ongenstown (d. 1886)
Occupation: Herd Documented: 1886 civil death registration; relationship to the Liscarton occupier not established

A Valuation Office Revision Book for Liscarton has been described as showing “Dead → to James Keary.” The original plot and page require verification, and the entry cannot yet be attributed to the James named in the following civil death registration.1 A civil death record cited for James Keary shows:
“James Keary, herd, married, died 11 May 1886, aged 65, Ongenstown; informant Luke Keary, present at death.”2

The original register records Luke as informant but does not state that he was James’s son. The death age implies birth around 1820–21, subject to normal uncertainty in reported ages. This death registration does not identify James as a son of Thomas Keary (d. 1857).

Regional economic context (not individual biography). The period included:

  • The Great Famine (1845–1852) affected County Meath. The circumstances of this particular James Keary during those years are not documented by the records presented here.
  • Land Reform (1870–1881) brought the Ulster Custom and later the Irish Land Acts, allowing tenants fair rents and fixity of tenure. Landholding arrangements for individual Keary families require the relevant leases and valuation records.

Religious and educational context. Catholic emancipation and the national-school system affected County Meath during the nineteenth century. The cited death registration does not establish James’s personal religious practices, schooling or literacy.

2 Thomas Keary of Ongenstown (d. 1909; identity unresolved)
Occupation: Herd Marital status: Bachelor Evidence: original 1909 civil death registration

Land-record caution. A reported Valuation Office annotation has not been verified against its original plot and page and cannot be associated with the Thomas Keary who died in 1909. No relationship between this Thomas, the Liscarton holding and the earlier Martry family is established.3

His death certificate reads:
“Thomas Keary, herd, bachelor, died 5 June 1909, aged 52, Ongenstown; informant Hugh Keary, brother, present at death.”4

The original death entry explicitly identifies Hugh Keary as Thomas’s brother. The Thomas recorded in Rose Keary’s Durhamstown household in the 1911 census was alive after this death and cannot be the deceased of 1909. The deceased’s parents and place within the wider Keary genealogy are not established by this entry.

Census Context. A 1901 Durhamstown household headed by Rose Keary records her children Thomas (38), Anne (36), Patrick (32) and Christopher (30). Hugh (78) was recorded in a separate household.5 A Thomas is also present with Rose in the original 1911 census; he cannot be the man who died in 1909. Building characteristics require separate inspection of the house-and-building return.

Economic Conditions. The decades 1870–1900 were marked by falling grain prices and rising livestock markets. The region’s shift from tillage to cattle fattening provides historical context, but the records assembled here do not establish a third uninterrupted father-to-son handover or the identity of the subsequent occupier.

3 Disputed James-and-Margaret household identification (1901–1911)
Evidence: census household transcriptions; later deaths and precise relationships under review

Earlier website drafts attributed census entries for James, Margaret, Thomas and James Keary to Durhamstown in 1901 and 1911. The original household images and identifiers supporting this transcription have not been provided or verified. These entries are not treated as established census facts in this edition.

The original 1911 Durhamstown form currently held in this research collection records Rose Keary, not Margaret, as the head of the relevant household. A purported 1930 death of a Thomas Keary is mentioned in previous notes, but no matching original death entry has been audited for this edition.6 Neither this claimed death nor the James-and-Margaret household is used to establish descent.

Agrarian Reform Context. The 1903 Wyndham Land Act provided a mechanism for tenant purchase. No original deed has been inspected that proves any specific Keary in this chapter purchased the holding in question.

4 Separate Luke Keary records: Ongenstown birth and Martry marriage
Ongenstown birth: father James Keary, mother Bridget (“Biddy”) formerly Coyle; Martry marriage: groom’s father William Keary

Luke’s birth was recorded as 30 December 1865 and registered in 1866. The registration names his father James and mother “Biddy Keary formerly Coyle.”7 Subsequent births — Patt (1868), Hugh (1870), Bridget (1873) — confirm the same parental pair.8–10 This Biddy/Bridget is thus securely identified as Bridget Coyle, wife of James Keary of Ongenstown.

A Luke Keary, aged 35, of Martry, married Mary Leonard on 1 January 1887. The marriage names his father as William Keary, whereas the 1865 birth names James Keary as father. The identity of these two Lukes has not been established. Mary’s father is named as Nicholas Leonard in the marriage entry.11 Separate birth entries record William (1888), Patrick (1893) and James (1897) with parents Luke Keary and Mary formerly Leonard. The cited William birth-register page does not establish an Elizabeth as his twin.12–15 William later married Elizabeth Smith (1925), naming Luke as father.16 The cited 1938 register page has not been verified as Luke’s death. The cited 1935 death entry records a Mary Keary as a spinster, with Patrick Keary described as brother; it cannot establish the death of Mary Leonard, wife of Luke.17–18

Interpretation. The Ongenstown births establish James Keary and Bridget Coyle as parents of the children named there. The Martry marriage establishes a Luke whose father was William Keary. These two source groups must remain separate unless identity can be proved.

5 Socio-Religious Context: The Catholic Revival and Rural Reform
Between 1850 and 1900 County Meath underwent major social change:

  • The Catholic Revival. Parish life developed during the nineteenth century. The original subscription lists cited in earlier versions have not been identified, so no named Keary participation is asserted.
  • Education. The national-school system expanded during the nineteenth century. Literacy for particular Keary household members should be reported only where it is recorded in their census entries or other primary documents.
  • Land League Activity. Land agitation and the Land Acts form part of the regional context. Individual Keary memberships are not established without the precise contemporary newspaper references.

These regional changes provide historical context. The records cited in this chapter do not establish whether any particular Keary family member participated in land agitation, purchased land or received a financial benefit from reform.
